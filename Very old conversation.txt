## What the BSP actually is

At the Econ department it's a two-stage thesis: **EC 495 (Stage I, 6 credits, Sem 7)** and **EC 497 (Stage II, 12 credits, Sem 8)** — 18 credits total, department core, both semesters. That's thesis scale, not term-paper scale. Stage I is essentially: nail the question, build the dataset, get preliminary results. Stage II is identification, robustness, writing.

The bar at this level isn't "did you find a profitable strategy." It's **do you have a credible identification strategy for a causal or well-posed empirical claim.** A backtest with a Sharpe ratio gets you a B. A clean natural experiment with a DiD and a falsification test gets you an A and possibly a working paper.

## The framing trap to avoid

Do **not** write "Is the Indian stock market weak-form efficient?" It's been done a hundred times with runs tests and ADF, it's unfalsifiable in practice (joint hypothesis problem), and no examiner will be impressed.

The good news: 2024–26 handed you the richest set of exogenous shocks Indian market microstructure has ever seen. SEBI's derivatives overhaul gives you real natural experiments where efficiency is the *outcome variable*, not the thesis statement.

## Seven ideas, roughly ranked

**1. The expiry-day migration as a DiD on price efficiency** ⭐
On 1 Sep 2025, NSE moved its entire F&O segment to Tuesday and BSE moved to Thursday. Same economy, same firms, two different expiry days. Compare intraday efficiency measures (variance ratios, 1-min return autocorrelation, Amihud illiquidity, strike-pinning frequency) for Nifty vs Sensex-linked names, before/after. Nobody has a full year of post-period data yet — you do, as of now. Highest novelty-per-unit-effort on this list.

**2. F&O ban period as a limits-to-arbitrage shock** ⭐⭐
My favourite. When a stock crosses 95% MWPL it enters the ban period and only position-reducing trades are allowed — a sharp, repeated, mechanically-triggered contraction in arbitrage capacity. Test whether futures-cash basis mispricing widens, whether PEAD deepens, whether the value/quality spread decays slower. Hundreds of events, free daily data from NSE, and it's a *uniquely Indian institution* — genuine contribution to the Shleifer-Vishny limits-to-arbitrage literature. Use the 95% threshold for an RD design and you have a very strong paper.

**3. Retail exit and noise-trader theory**
Minimum notional was raised to ~₹15 lakh from the ₹5–7 lakh range, effective November 2024, plus upfront premium collection from 1 Feb 2025. This is an exogenous purge of small retail. DeLong-Shleifer-Summers-Waldmann says removing noise traders should improve efficiency; the liquidity-provision view says the opposite. Cross-sectional DiD using pre-reform retail F&O share as treatment intensity. You get to adjudicate between two canonical models.

**4. Losing weekly expiries and informed trading in options**
Weekly expiries were retained only for Nifty 50, while Bank Nifty, Fin Nifty and Midcap Nifty were restricted to monthly and quarterly cycles after SEBI's November 2024 directive. Easley-O'Hara-Srinivas says options are where informed traders go. Do Bank Nifty constituents become slower to incorporate information relative to Nifty non-financials? Clean treatment/control split.

**5. Staggered T+0 rollout**
T+0 was rolled out to the top 500 stocks in phases. Staggered adoption by market-cap tranche → staggered DiD, but you must use Callaway–Sant'Anna or Sun–Abraham estimators (two-way FE is biased here). Demonstrating you know that is itself a signal of sophistication. Outcomes: spreads, cash-futures arbitrage speed, short-horizon reversal.

**6. Anomalies: risk premium or mispricing?**
Reuses your existing F&O long-short infrastructure directly. Engelberg-McLean-Pontiff test: if anomaly returns concentrate disproportionately on earnings announcement days, it's expectation errors, not risk. Run it on your 45-signal library. Lowest marginal cost for you, and it converts a backtest into a real research question.

**7. Do index inclusion effects still exist?**
Nifty semi-annual rebalances, tests the downward-sloping demand curve hypothesis, and you can ask whether the effect has decayed as passive AUM grew. Solid, safe fallback — very feasible data-wise, slightly less exciting.

## Program A: Limits to Arbitrage in Indian Equities — Measurement, Sources, Consequences

The organising question: **why do mispricings persist in India, and what does that tell us about who the marginal investor is?** India is unusual — no true cash short-selling, a thin SLB market, hard position caps, and an F&O ban mechanism. That's a distinct arbitrage environment from the US, so almost every US result is worth re-asking.

- **Ch 1 — Construct an Indian arbitrage-constraint index.** Combine F&O ban days, MWPL utilisation, futures basis dispersion, idiosyncratic volatility, ownership breadth from quarterly shareholding patterns, delivery percentage. - **Ch 2 — Validate it.** Does the index predict the magnitude and half-life of known mispricings (cash-futures basis violations, PEAD, closed-end fund style discounts)?
- **Ch 3 — Cross-section of anomaly returns.** Fama-MacBeth: do your 45 signals earn more in high-constraint names? This is the direct Stambaugh-Yu-Yuan test, and it reuses your existing pipeline wholesale.
- **Ch 4 — Causal identification.** The 95% MWPL threshold as a regression discontinuity; the 2024–26 SEBI reforms as DiD shocks to constraint intensity.
- **Ch 5 — Asset pricing implications.** Is the constraint priced? Does a mimicking factor improve the Indian factor model?

Techniques span: index construction, panel FE, Fama-MacBeth, RD, DiD, GRS tests. That's a thesis with genuine range.

## Program B: The Retail Transformation of Indian Markets, 2019–2026

Organising question: **India added tens of millions of retail participants in five years. What did that do to prices, and did SEBI's response help?**

- **Ch 2 — Descriptive facts.** Where did retail concentrate — small caps, lottery-like stocks, high-IV names? Test Kumar's gambling-preference hypothesis on Indian data.
- **Ch 3 — Efficiency effects.** Does retail intensity predict return autocorrelation, reversal magnitude, post-announcement drift? Noise traders vs liquidity providers.

## Program A: Limits to Arbitrage

**Literature check.** I searched for academic work treating India's F&O ban period as an arbitrage-constraint shock and found essentially nothing. There's a large international limits-to-arbitrage literature (Shleifer-Vishny, Gromb-Vayanos, Ofek-Richardson-Whitelaw on put-call parity under short-sale constraints, Stambaugh-Yu-Yuan on the anomaly cross-section), and separate work on position limits in Korea and Taiwan — but the Indian MWPL mechanism is essentially unstudied. That's a genuine gap, not a "nobody's written it in the last two years" gap.

**Data.** Fully sufficient on public sources. NSE publishes the daily ban-period list as a downloadable CSV, and the trigger and release rules are mechanical — entry at 95% of MWPL, release below 80%. MWPL itself is set as either 20% of free-float equity capital excluding promoter holdings, or 30 times the previous month's average volume. Combined with F&O bhavcopy OI, you can reconstruct utilisation daily.

**The problem you must solve.** A stock enters the ban *because* speculative interest crowded into it — which is precisely when mispricing is likely anyway. Naive "ban → wider basis" is reverse causality. And the RD at 95% is weaker than it looks: the running variable is manipulable and anticipated, so a McCrary density test will almost certainly show bunching.

**The fix, and it's the crux of whether this project works.** MWPL is recomputed periodically off free-float and *prior-month* average volume. So the denominator moves for reasons unrelated to today's speculative pressure — the same open interest can be in-ban or not depending on last month's volume. That gives you variation in ban status that is plausibly orthogonal to current mispricing. Build your identification around denominator shifts, not around the threshold crossing. If you can make that work, you have a real paper. If you can't, you have a correlational study.

**Second complication.** The mechanism changed mid-sample twice: from October 2025, risk-reducing trades became permitted during the ban, and from December 2025, open interest moved to a delta-based future-equivalent measure with intraday MWPL monitoring rather than day-end checks. That's annoying — but it's also two more clean policy shocks to the *intensity* of the constraint. Turn the bug into a chapter.

**Where it's weakest.** Only ~220 F&O stocks. Chapter 5 (is the constraint a priced factor?) will be underpowered — GRS tests on that cross-section over a decade won't give you much. Demote it to a robustness section. Also, put-call parity measures need reliable option prices, and closing settlement prices for OTM strikes in Indian single-stock options are stale and thin. Restrict to near-ATM and the futures basis.

## Program B: The Retail Transformation

I want to like this one — it's the better *economics* question. But it has a measurement problem I think is close to disqualifying.

**Crowdedness.** Very high, and at low quality. The searches returned a large volume of descriptive papers in minor journals — "retail vs institutional investors in India," "the retail investing paradox," and so on — mostly correlational, using aggregate SEBI/NSE/AMFI series. Being the twentieth such paper is a bad position, and being distinguishable requires exactly the identification the data won't support.

**The measurement problem.** Participant-wise OI is published daily and splits FII/DII/Pro/Client across index futures, stock futures, and index and stock options — but it is a **market-level aggregate**, not per-stock. You cannot construct a firm-level daily retail intensity from it. Your only firm-level retail measure is the quarterly shareholding pattern (individual shareholders up to ₹2 lakh nominal capital), which measures *ownership*, not *trading*. The retail F&O phenomenon is overwhelmingly non-ownership speculation. Your treatment variable would not measure the thing your treatment acts on.

**The DiD problem is worse.** Nearly all the 2024-26 reforms are *index* derivative reforms — lot size notional, weekly expiry consolidation, upfront premium collection. Index-level treatment means N of one or two. No cross-section means no difference-in-differences, which leaves you with time-series event studies over a window that also contains large FII flow reversals, global rate moves, and the Jane Street shock. Attribution becomes hand-waving.

**The welfare chapter is a trap.** SEBI's estimates rest on account-level regulatory data you will never see. Producing a worse version of a number the regulator already published, using public aggregates, is not a contribution — it's a comparison you lose.

**Program A, clearly.** Not because the question is more interesting — B's is — but because A is the one where the data can actually carry the identification. Research projects fail on the gap between the question and the evidence, and B's gap is structural and unfixable with public data.
